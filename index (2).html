    Gallery one: the Moon

    The first footprints, the forgotten machines

    More than half a century ago, humans did something that had once seemed impossible. They landed astronauts on another world.

    But astronauts couldn't explore the Moon with their feet alone. They needed technology: scientific instruments, cameras, communication equipment and, on the later Apollo missions, a car. Imagine driving on a world without roads.

    The astronauts came home. The science stayed.

    Silent since the last crew left   Apollo 17 lifted off from the Moon on 14 December 1972. About 384,400 km from Earth

    Gallery two: Mars

    The little machine that refused to give up

    Now travel millions of kilometres away. Welcome to Mars, a planet that has fascinated people for generations.

    Exploring it is not easy. There is no astronaut waiting to repair a broken machine. No mechanic to replace a damaged wheel. No human hand to clean its instruments. Everything has to be designed to work remotely.

    NASA sent two robotic twins: Spirit and Opportunity.

    Silent since Opportunity's last signal   A global dust storm blocked its sunlight in June 2018. 55 to 400 million km from Earth

    Gallery three: deep space

    The travellers that never came home

    Our final destination is different. There is no ground beneath us. No mountains, no roads, no atmosphere. Only the endless dark.

    Unlike the rovers on the Moon and Mars, the Voyagers are not simply abandoned equipment. They kept going long after their planetary tour ended, and that is what makes them extraordinary.

    Two machines from Earth are carrying a message from our planet into the vastness of space.

    Voyager 1 has been travelling for   Launched 5 September 1977. Still sending data home. More than 24 billion km from Earth

      The question nobody asks

      Why do we leave these machines behind?

      Open each reason.

      Every kilogram you bring home needs extra propellant to land, to take off again and to fly back. That propellant has weight too, which needs even more propellant. The numbers snowball.

      So the Apollo crews spent their limited return capacity on what mattered most: more than 380 kg of Moon rock across the six landings.

      Returning a spacecraft means a bigger rocket, more hardware and years of added cost. And for a machine like a rover, most of its value is already coming home as data, photographs and measurements.

      The knowledge travels back. The metal stays where it is.

      Some hardware is designed to remain on the surface after its mission. Other machines stop working because of dust, cold, ageing batteries or a failed part, with no one nearby to fix them.

      Spirit and Opportunity never had a way back. They were built to live and work exactly where they landed.

      Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 are still operating in interstellar space, talking to Earth across more than 24 billion kilometres. They are not abandoned. They are on the longest journey humans have ever started.

      Every mission teaches us something. These machines help us build better spacecraft, develop stronger technology and prepare for future human exploration.

      And perhaps one day, the next generation of explorers will stand beside the old Apollo rover tracks and the silent Mars landers, and see that these machines were never just metal, wires and computers. They were the tools that helped humanity answer some of its biggest questions.
